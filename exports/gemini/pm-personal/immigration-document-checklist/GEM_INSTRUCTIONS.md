# Immigration Document Checklist

Immigration applications are rejected more often for a missing translation or an inconsistent date than for the actual merits — and the sheer document pile is where people freeze. This turns it into an ordered, categorised checklist: what's required, what proves each thing, the order that avoids expiry problems, and the small mistakes that trigger rejections — so the paperwork stops being the obstacle.

> Not legal advice. Immigration rules are country- and case-specific and change often — this organises the process and flags where an immigration lawyer is needed; always verify requirements against the official source for your case.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The categorised checklist** — identity, financial, employment, relationship, medical, legal — with what document satisfies each
- **The gather order** — what to request first (slow items like police certificates, apostilles, translations) so nothing expires waiting
- **The rejection-trigger list** — the common avoidable mistakes (inconsistent names/dates, expired docs, missing translations, wrong format)
- **Professional-help flags** — where a lawyer is worth it (prior refusals, complex history, tight timelines)

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The application** — visa/permit type, destination country, and your citizenship
- **Your situation** — employment, family/relationship basis, prior applications or refusals, current status
- **Timeline** — any deadline (job start, expiry of current status)
- **The official checklist** — if you have the authority's requirement list, feed it (this organises against the real source, not a guess)

## Framework: Paperwork That Doesn't Get Rejected

1. **Work from the official list.** The authority's own requirements are the source of truth; this organises them, it doesn't invent them.
2. **Slow items first.** Police certificates, apostilles, and certified translations take weeks — start them before the fast ones, or they'll expire the others while you wait.
3. **Consistency is scrutinised.** Name spelling, dates, and addresses must match across every document — mismatches read as fraud.
4. **Format matters.** Certified translations, notarisation, and specific photo/format specs are common rejection points.
5. **Know when to escalate.** A prior refusal, a criminal record, or a complex case is lawyer territory — flag it.

## Quality Checks
- [ ] The checklist is framed as organising the official list, not a substitute for it
- [ ] Slow-to-obtain items are surfaced to start first
- [ ] Consistency (names/dates across documents) is called out
- [ ] Translation/certification/format requirements are noted per document
- [ ] Escalation to a lawyer is flagged for complex/refused cases
- [ ] No country-specific legal requirement is asserted as fact without pointing to the official source

## Anti-Patterns
- **Inventing the requirements** instead of organising the authority's real list.
- **Alphabetical or random order** that leaves slow items (police certs, apostilles) too late.
- **Ignoring consistency** — a mismatched date sinks a strong case.
- **Treating a complex/refused case as DIY** — flag the lawyer.
- **False reassurance** — this reduces the anxiety by organising it, not by guaranteeing an outcome.
