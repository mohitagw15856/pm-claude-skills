# Rabbit Hole Rescue Skill

Watching someone you love disappear into conspiracy theories, misinformation, or
extremism is uniquely painful, and almost everything instinct tells you to do —
send the debunk, argue the facts, mock the belief — makes it worse, because the
belief isn't really about facts. It's usually meeting a need: belonging, certainty,
feeling special or in-the-know, a sense of control in a scary world. This skill drops
the losing game of fact-warfare and runs the approach researchers and exit-counselors
actually use: preserve the relationship (your influence lives entirely in the
relationship), stay curious instead of combative, and plant small doubts rather than
demand surrender. The realistic goal isn't winning the argument tonight — it's keeping
the door open so they have somewhere to come back to.

## What This Skill Produces

- A **conversation approach** for the specific person: how to engage the belief
  without attacking them, the questions that invite reflection instead of defense, and
  how to affirm the relationship even across the divide
- A **what-not-to-do list**: the instinctive moves (debunk-dumping, ridicule, ultimatums,
  "how can you believe that") that entrench them, and why each backfires
- A **realistic goal-set**: staged and honest — keep the relationship, keep the door
  open, plant a doubt, understand the underlying need — instead of the fantasy of a
  single conversation that snaps them out of it
- **Your own boundaries and wellbeing**: what to do when the belief is hateful or the
  conversations are hurting you — because keeping the door open never means accepting
  abuse or dehydrating yourself trying to save them

## Required Inputs

Ask for (if not already provided):
- Who it is, the relationship, and how far in they are (dabbling vs deep, and whether
  it's harming them or others)
- What they believe and — importantly — what you think it's *doing* for them (belonging?
  certainty? feeling important? fear?)
- What's been tried and how it went (usually arguing; usually badly)
- What the user actually wants and can sustain — and where their own limits are
  (hateful content, their own mental health)

## Framework

1. **Understand the need, not just the belief.** Rabbit holes recruit people through
   unmet needs — community, certainty in chaos, significance, distrust born of real
   grievance. The belief is the symptom; the need is the hook. You can't argue someone
   out of a need with facts. Identify what it's giving them; that's what any real
   off-ramp has to also provide.
2. **Protect the relationship above all — it's your only leverage.** People leave these
   spirals through relationships with people who stayed kind, not through being
   defeated in debate. Every argument that damages the relationship reduces your
   influence to zero and pushes them deeper into the group that's still "loyal." The
   prime directive: don't become another person who left them.
3. **Get curious, not combative.** Replace assertions with questions asked in genuine
   good faith: "what convinced you?", "is there anything that would change your mind?",
   "how do you know that source is right?" — Socratic, not gotcha. Sincere curiosity
   lowers defenses; it also occasionally surfaces the doubt they've been hiding even
   from themselves.
4. **Plant seeds; don't demand the harvest.** The goal of a single conversation is at
   most one small crack — a question they can't quite answer, a fact that doesn't fit,
   a moment of feeling loved-anyway. Not a recantation. Entrenchment comes from being
   cornered; doubt grows in private, later, when they're not defending. Leave, don't
   press.
5. **Guard your own wellbeing and draw the hard line.** Keeping the door open is not
   infinite tolerance. If the belief is hateful, targets others, or the person becomes
   abusive, boundaries come first: you can love someone and refuse to host their
   bigotry, and you can step back from conversations that are hurting you. The skill
   names when the healthy move is distance, not more rescue — and that walking away
   from an adult's choices is sometimes the only sane option, and not a failure.

## Output Format

```
## What the belief is doing for them
[The underlying need — belonging / certainty / significance / grievance / fear]

## Your approach (connection-first)
[How to engage the belief without attacking the person · the curious questions ·
affirming the relationship across the divide]

## Do NOT (and why it backfires)
[Debunk-dumping · ridicule · ultimatums · "how can you believe that" — each with the
entrenchment mechanism]

## The realistic goal (staged)
[Keep the relationship → keep the door open → plant one doubt → understand the need —
NOT win tonight]

## Your limits
[When the content is hateful / the person is abusive / this is hurting you → the
boundary, and permission to step back]
```

## Quality Checks

- [ ] The approach targets the underlying need, not just the false belief
- [ ] Relationship-preservation is framed as the source of all influence
- [ ] The what-not-to-do list explains the backfire mechanism, not just "don't"
- [ ] The goal is realistic and staged — no fantasy of one conversation curing it
- [ ] The user's own boundaries and wellbeing are protected, including permission to
      step back and the hard line on hateful content

## Anti-Patterns

- [ ] Do not supply debunk scripts or fact-arsenal as the primary tool — fact-warfare
      entrenches; this skill exists because that approach fails
- [ ] Do not counsel ridicule, cornering, or ultimatums — they push people deeper
- [ ] Do not promise the person can be argued back — set the honest, staged goal
- [ ] Do not counsel infinite tolerance — hateful belief and abuse get boundaries, and
      the user's wellbeing outranks the rescue
- [ ] Do not treat radicalization-with-violence-risk as a conversation problem — name
      plainly when it needs professional help, a support org for families, or safety
      authorities
