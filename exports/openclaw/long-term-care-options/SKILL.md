# Long-Term Care Options

Choosing care for an aging or ill loved one is one of the hardest decisions a family faces — emotionally fraught, expensive, and full of confusing options. This maps the landscape: what the main levels of care actually are and who each suits, how to match them to your person's real needs and budget, what to look for (and the red flags) when evaluating providers, and how to involve the person with dignity. It's a decision map, not medical or financial advice.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The options explained** — the main levels in plain language: in-home care (from occasional help to live-in), adult day programs, assisted living, memory care, and nursing/skilled care — and who each is for
- **A needs match** — which options fit the person's actual care level (daily-living help vs. medical vs. dementia care), budget, and preferences
- **Evaluation questions & red flags** — what to ask and observe when touring/vetting providers (staffing, safety, cleanliness, culture, turnover, reviews, inspection records)
- **Cost & funding to research** — the (jurisdiction-specific) cost ranges and funding routes to look into, flagged to verify
- **Involving the person** — how to include them in the decision and preserve dignity, even when capacity is limited
- **A boundary** — this maps options; medical needs assessment and financial/legal specifics need professionals

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The person's needs** — physical care, medical needs, cognitive status (dementia?), and how much help daily
- **Their wishes** — what they want, and their capacity to be involved
- **The family situation** — who's available, proximity, and the budget
- **The trigger** — a health change, a crisis, safety concerns, or planning ahead
- **Region** — for the (educational) cost/funding pointers

## Framework: Match Need To Level, Vet Carefully

1. **Explain the levels clearly.** From in-home help through assisted living to nursing care, describe what each provides and the kind of need it suits — the terms confuse everyone.
2. **Match to real needs.** The right option depends on care level (help with daily living vs. skilled medical vs. memory care), not just preference or cost — assess honestly.
3. **Weigh the trade-offs.** In-home preserves familiarity but has limits; facilities offer more support but are a big change — surface the trade-offs for this person.
4. **Vet providers hard.** Give the questions and red flags: staffing ratios and turnover, safety and cleanliness, the culture/warmth, reviews, and any inspection/regulatory records.
5. **Research cost and funding.** Costs are high and funding is jurisdiction-specific — point to the ranges and funding routes to investigate, flagged to verify locally.
6. **Involve the person with dignity.** Include them as much as their capacity allows; this is their life. And bring in professional assessment for medical/financial/legal specifics.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Recommending a level** without assessing real care needs.
- **Ignoring the trade-offs** of each option.
- **No provider red flags** — how families end up somewhere bad.
- **Asserting costs/funding** as universal.
- **Deciding over the person** rather than with them.
