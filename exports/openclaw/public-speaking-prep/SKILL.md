# Public-Speaking Prep

A good talk isn't about being a naturally gifted speaker — it's preparation: a clear core message, a structure the audience can follow, a strong open and close, and enough rehearsal that nerves have less room. This preps your specific talk end to end, from what you're actually trying to say to handling the shaky-hands moment before you start.

## What This Skill Produces

- **A message-first structure** — your one core point and the few supporting ideas, shaped for *this* audience (not a data dump)
- **A strong open and close** — an opening that earns attention and a closing that lands the message (the two most-remembered parts)
- **Delivery guidance** — pace, pauses, emphasis, and whether to speak from notes, bullets, or (rarely) a script
- **A nerves plan** — practical techniques to manage anxiety before and during
- **A rehearsal approach** — how to practice effectively (out loud, timed, ideally recorded or to a person)
- **Q&A prep** — anticipating questions and handling the ones you can't answer
- **Slides/visual notes** — if relevant, keeping them supportive, not a script on a wall

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The talk** — topic, purpose (inform/persuade/inspire), and length
- **The audience** — who they are, what they know, and what they care about
- **The setting** — formal/informal, in-person/virtual, slides or not, Q&A
- **Your experience & nerves** — comfort level and where you struggle
- **Constraints** — time to prepare, any content that's fixed

## Framework: Message First, Then Deliver

1. **Nail the one message.** Decide the single thing the audience should remember, and build everything to serve it — clarity beats cramming.
2. **Structure for the listener.** A simple, followable arc (tell them where you're going, make your points with support, land it) — audiences can't re-read a talk.
3. **Engineer the open and close.** Open with something that earns attention (a question, story, or stakes) and close with a clear, memorable takeaway — these stick most.
4. **Deliver like a human.** Pace with pauses, emphasis, and natural phrasing; speak from notes/bullets rather than reading a script; keep slides supportive.
5. **Manage the nerves.** Preparation reduces them most; add practical techniques (breathing, reframing, a strong start memorized) for the moment.
6. **Rehearse for real.** Practice out loud and timed — to a person or recorded — not silently in your head; refine from what you notice.
7. **Prep Q&A.** Anticipate likely questions and have a graceful way to handle ones you can't answer.

## Output Format

### Talk prep: [topic/purpose] · [audience] · [length] · [setting]

**Core message:** [the one thing they should remember].
**Structure:** open → [point 1 · 2 · 3 with support] → close.
**Open:** [attention-earning hook]. **Close:** [memorable takeaway].
**Delivery:** [pace/pauses · notes vs script · slides supportive].
**Nerves:** [prep + in-the-moment techniques].
**Rehearse:** [out loud, timed, to a person/recorded].
**Q&A:** [likely questions + handling the unknown ones].

## Quality Checks
- [ ] Built around one clear core message for the audience
- [ ] Has a followable structure
- [ ] Includes a strong, specific open and close
- [ ] Gives concrete delivery guidance (notes over script, pacing)
- [ ] Includes a practical nerves-management plan
- [ ] Includes an effective rehearsal approach and Q&A prep

## Anti-Patterns
- **A data dump** with no core message.
- **Reading a script** word-for-word.
- **Weak open/close** — burying the memorable moments.
- **Slides as the script** — walls of text read aloud.
- **"I'll wing it"** — no real, out-loud rehearsal.
- **Ignoring Q&A** until caught out.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "I have to give a 10-minute presentation at work — help me prepare."
- "Help me prep a best-man speech / a conference talk."
- "How do I structure a persuasive presentation?"
- "I get terrible nerves speaking — help me manage them for a talk."
- "Prepare me for the Q&A after my presentation."
