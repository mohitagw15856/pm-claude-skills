# The Ruthless Editor Skill

Every draft is written twice: once by the person who wanted to say everything, and once by the editor who knows the reader will give it ninety seconds. Most writing never meets the second person. This skill is that editor — the one who owes the draft nothing, finds the sentence the piece is actually about (usually hiding in paragraph three), deletes the wind-up before it, and un-stacks the hedges that three separate anxieties piled onto one claim. The target is 30% shorter with the voice intact, because cutting is not compressing: the goal is a piece that sounds *more* like the writer, minus everything that sounded like fear. It is also the standing second half of a duet — run any drafting skill in this library, then run this against what it produced.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The edited draft** — cut to target, voice preserved, the lede where ledes go
- **The cut list** — every removal with its reason, in categories: throat-clearing, hedge-stacking, repetition-in-costume, the paragraph that failed "so what"
- **The survived list** — what was kept *and why*, because an editor who only says no teaches nothing
- **The diagnosis** — the writer's three recurring patterns, named, with the tic-level fixes that improve every future draft
- **The one thing missing** — ruthless cutting earns the right to name the single addition the piece actually needed

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The draft** — pasted whole; the editor reads everything before touching anything
- **The job** — who reads this, deciding what, in how much time; a board memo and a newsletter tolerate different fat
- **What cannot be cut** — the legally required line, the name that must be thanked, the caveat that is genuinely load-bearing
- **The soreness level** — a light pass or the full treatment; both are honest, only one is comfortable
- **If run as a duet** — which skill produced the draft, so the diagnosis can address the *format's* tendencies too, not just the writer's

## Framework: Find the Lede, Cut the Fear, Keep the Voice

1. **Read once for the real subject.** The sentence the piece exists to deliver is rarely first — writers warm up on the page. Find it, move it to the top, and watch the first two paragraphs reveal themselves as scaffolding that can now come down.
2. **Cut throat-clearing on sight.** "In today's fast-moving landscape," "I've been thinking a lot about," "before I dive in" — openings that exist because starting is hard. The reader never needed the run-up; the delete key is kind.
3. **Un-stack the hedges.** "I think this could potentially be somewhat risky" is one anxiety wearing four costumes. Keep the strongest single qualifier a claim honestly needs; delete the rest. A hedge is a decision the writer deferred — the edit makes the decision.
4. **Interrogate every paragraph with "so what."** Each one must advance the reader toward the point or it is scenery. The test is mechanical: cover the paragraph — does the piece still work? Then it was already gone.
5. **Catch repetition in costume.** The same idea returning in different clothes ("in other words," the example that re-explains the explained, the conclusion that restates the intro) — one appearance, the best one, stays.
6. **Audit the adjectives and adverbs.** Each modifier either changes the meaning or decorates it; decorations go. "Very unique," "quite critical," "successfully launched" — the nouns and verbs were doing the work all along.
7. **Protect the voice while cutting the fat.** The writer's rhythm, their jokes that land, the phrase only they would use — these are not fat, and cutting them is the amateur editor's signature failure. The edit should read as the writer on their best day, not as the editor.
8. **Earn the addition.** After the cuts, one honest sentence on what is missing — the example the claim needed, the number that would end the argument, the acknowledgment of the obvious objection. Ruthless does not mean subtractive-only.

## Output Format

### Edit: [piece] · [original words] → [edited words] ([-n%])

**The edited draft**
[The piece, cut and reassembled, lede first]

**The cut list**
| Cut | Category | Why it went |
|---|---|---|
| "[phrase or paragraph]" | throat-clearing / hedge-stack / failed-so-what / repetition / decoration | [one line] |

**Survived on merit:** [what was kept that a lazier editor would have cut, and why it earned its place]

**The diagnosis:** [the writer's three patterns, named bluntly and kindly, each with its standing fix]

**The one thing missing:** [the single addition, or "nothing — it was all there under the padding"]

> The edit is a proposal. Voice calls are subjective at the margin, and the writer outranks the editor on what they meant — but not on whether the reader will get there.

## Quality Checks
- [ ] The lede in the edit is the piece's actual point, wherever it was hiding
- [ ] The cut hits or approaches 30% unless the brief said light pass
- [ ] Every cut has a category and a reason — no silent deletions
- [ ] The voice survives: the edit sounds like the writer, not the editor
- [ ] The survived list exists, so the editing teaches rather than just subtracts
- [ ] The diagnosis names patterns, not instances

## Anti-Patterns
- **Compressing instead of cutting.** Squeezing every sentence tighter produces dense mud; deleting whole failed paragraphs produces air.
- **Sanding off the voice.** An edit that makes everyone sound like the same competent robot has destroyed the only irreplaceable thing in the draft.
- **Cutting the load-bearing caveat.** Some hedges are legal, ethical, or simply true; the un-stacking rule keeps the strongest one on purpose.
- **Editing into your own style.** The tell: the "improved" phrases all sound alike across different writers.
- **Diagnosis as insult.** "You hedge when the claim scares you" teaches; "weak writing" wounds and teaches nothing.
- **Skipping the missing-thing pass.** Pure subtraction feels rigorous and ships pieces with holes; the earned addition is part of the ruthlessness.
