# Boundary-Setting Scripts

A boundary is only real if you can say it out loud and hold it — which is exactly what most people can't do, so they hint, resent, and explode instead. This helps you name the boundary you actually need, gives you warm-but-firm words to state it (no over-explaining, no apologizing it into nothing), and prepares you for the pushback — because the guilt-trip and the "you're being difficult" are coming, and folding at them means you never had a boundary.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The boundary, clarified** — what you actually need to change, stated as a clear limit (not a vague wish)
- **A warm-but-firm script** — the words to say it kindly and directly, without over-justifying or apologizing it away
- **The hold-the-line plan** — responses to the pushback, guilt-trips, and "you've changed / you're overreacting"
- **The consequence** — what you'll do if the boundary isn't respected (a boundary with no follow-through is a suggestion)
- **A relationship-aware tone** — calibrated to who it's with (a boss ≠ a partner ≠ a parent)

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The situation** — who, and what they're doing that you need to change
- **What you need** — the specific limit (time, behavior, topic, availability)
- **The relationship** — friend, family, partner, coworker, boss (changes tone and leverage)
- **The dynamic** — how they usually react, and whether you've raised it before
- **Your goal** — change the behavior while keeping the relationship, or a firmer stance

## Framework: Name It, State It, Hold It

1. **Clarify the actual boundary.** Turn a vague frustration into a specific limit — "I need X to stop" or "I'm not available for Y" — because you can't state what you haven't defined.
2. **State it warm and firm.** Lead with care if the relationship warrants, then the boundary plainly — and stop. Over-explaining and apologizing invite negotiation.
3. **Don't justify it to death.** "No" is a complete sentence; a boundary doesn't require a defense that gives them something to argue with.
4. **Prepare for pushback.** Guilt, anger, and "you're being unreasonable" are common — a calm broken-record restatement holds the line without a fight.
5. **Attach a consequence.** Decide what you'll actually do if it's ignored (leave the conversation, reduce contact, escalate) — and be prepared to follow through, or it's not a boundary.
6. **Calibrate to the relationship.** The words for a boss differ from a parent from a partner — tune tone and firmness accordingly.

## Output Format

### Boundary with: [person] · you need: [the limit]

**The boundary, clear:** [the specific limit].
**Script (warm + firm):**
> [Care if warranted] + [the boundary, plainly] + [stop — no over-explaining].
**When they push back:** "[calm restatement]" — hold, don't debate or over-justify.
**If it's not respected:** [the consequence you'll follow through on].
**Tone for this relationship:** [calibrated to boss/partner/parent/friend].

## Quality Checks
- [ ] Turns a vague frustration into a specific, statable boundary
- [ ] Script is warm and firm, without over-explaining or apologizing
- [ ] Includes hold-the-line responses to pushback/guilt
- [ ] Attaches a real consequence with follow-through
- [ ] Tone is calibrated to the specific relationship

## Anti-Patterns
- **Over-explaining/apologizing** the boundary into nothing.
- **Hinting** instead of stating it.
- **No plan for the pushback** — folding at the first guilt-trip.
- **A boundary with no consequence** — just a suggestion.
- **Same tone** for a boss and a parent.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "How do I set a boundary with my mom about dropping by unannounced?"
- "I need to tell a friend I can't keep lending money — what do I say?"
- "A coworker keeps dumping work on me. Help me set a limit."
- "How do I say no without feeling guilty?"
- "Someone keeps overstepping and I don't know how to stop it."
