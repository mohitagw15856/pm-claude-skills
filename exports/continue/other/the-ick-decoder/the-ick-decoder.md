# The Ick Decoder Skill

"The ick" — the sudden inexplicable repulsion toward someone you were into — is one
of dating's great confusions, because it comes in three completely different flavors
wearing the same costume. Sometimes it's your instincts correctly flagging a real
incompatibility or red flag. Sometimes it's an anxious/avoidant self-sabotage pattern
that manufactures a reason to flee anyone who gets close. And sometimes it's a
superficial ick (he ran for the bus funny) that has nothing to do with anything. Most
dating advice validates whichever one you want to hear. This skill instead
interrogates the *specific* ick to tell which flavor it is — because dumping someone
great over an avoidant spiral and ignoring a real red flag are opposite mistakes with
the same feeling.

## What This Skill Produces

- A **decode of the specific ick**: what exactly triggered it, and what category it's
  pointing at (values/incompatibility, safety/red-flag, attachment-pattern, or genuinely
  superficial)
- A **verdict**: real red flag (trust it, act) · genuine incompatibility (valid, no
  villain) · self-sabotage pattern (worth examining before acting) · superficial (let
  it pass) — with the reasoning, not a rubber stamp
- A **pattern check**: whether this ick fits a repeating cycle across the user's dating
  history (the tell of self-sabotage)
- A **next move**: what to actually do — end it cleanly, raise the real concern, sit
  with it a beat, or notice-and-let-go

## Required Inputs

Ask for (if not already provided):
- The specific ick — the exact moment or thing, concretely ("he texted good morning
  three days running", not "something felt off")
- How the person has actually treated the user (the behavior, separate from the ick)
- The user's dating history pattern: do they often find a reason to end things right
  when it's getting real? do they ignore red flags for too long? (both are patterns)
- How they felt *before* the ick — into it, ambivalent, already looking for an exit?

## Framework

1. **Interrogate the specific, not the vibe.** "The ick" as a vague feeling is
   undecodable; the specific trigger is everything. Get concrete — the exact behavior,
   moment, or trait — because "he was too available" and "he lied about where he was"
   are wildly different data wearing the same discomfort.
2. **Sort into the four flavors.** (a) *Red flag*: the ick is pointing at behavior
   that predicts harm — control, dishonesty, cruelty, boundary-pushing. Trust it. (b)
   *Real incompatibility*: a genuine values/life/attraction mismatch — valid, and
   nobody's the villain. (c) *Attachment pattern*: the ick reliably appears right as
   intimacy deepens, especially with people who are actually kind and available — the
   signature of avoidant self-sabotage. (d) *Superficial*: a texture/quirk thing that
   your brain inflated — usually passes.
3. **Run the pattern check — it's the tiebreaker.** The single most useful question:
   does this ick fit a cycle? If the user consistently gets the ick from stable,
   kind, into-them partners right when it's getting real, the ick is more about the
   user's nervous system than the person. If they consistently override real red flags
   until it blows up, that's the opposite pattern. History decodes the present.
4. **Distinguish "he's available" ick from "he's unsafe" ick.** A crucial split:
   avoidant patterns often manufacture icks precisely about a partner's warmth,
   availability, and effort (the exact good qualities) — because closeness itself is
   the threat. A red flag is about *behavior that would hurt you*. Same feeling,
   opposite meaning; name which one this is.
5. **Give an honest next move, not a permission slip.** The skill won't just tell the
   user what they want to hear. Red flag → here's how to end it cleanly and safely.
   Incompatibility → it's okay to leave, no cruelty needed. Pattern → before you bolt,
   here's the experiment (name it to yourself, sit a week, see if it's the person or
   the closing distance). Superficial → let it go, or don't, but know that's what it is.

## Output Format

```
## Your specific ick
[The exact trigger, stated concretely]

## What flavor is it?
[Red flag / real incompatibility / attachment pattern / superficial — with the
reasoning that places it there]

## The pattern check
[Does this fit a cycle in your history? Available-people ick vs override-red-flags —
which, if either?]

## Available-ick vs unsafe-ick
[Is this about them being warm/into you, or about behavior that would hurt you?]

## Your honest next move
[End cleanly (+ how) / leave without a villain / run the pattern experiment first /
notice and let it pass — the real one, not the flattering one]
```

## Quality Checks

- [ ] The decode works from the *specific* trigger, not a vague vibe
- [ ] It genuinely distinguishes the four flavors and commits to a verdict with reasoning
- [ ] The pattern/history check is run — it's the tiebreaker for self-sabotage
- [ ] The available-ick vs unsafe-ick distinction is explicitly drawn
- [ ] The next move is honest, not whichever answer the user was fishing for

## Anti-Patterns

- [ ] Do not rubber-stamp the user's preferred conclusion — the value is telling
      self-sabotage from a real flag, which sometimes means the unwelcome answer
- [ ] Do not pathologize every ick as avoidance OR validate every ick as instinct —
      both lazy takes; the specific trigger decides
- [ ] Do not talk anyone into staying with someone who treats them badly — a red flag
      is a red flag, and safety always overrides "maybe it's your pattern"
- [ ] Do not talk anyone out of leaving someone they're simply not compatible with —
      "no villain" is a valid ending
- [ ] Do not play therapist for deep attachment wounds — name when a pattern might
      deserve real therapeutic work, and leave it there
