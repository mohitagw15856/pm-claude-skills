# In-Law Boundary Scripts

Boundaries with extended family are hard because you want to protect your peace *and* the relationships. The trick is to be warm and clear at once, and — crucially — for the couple to be united, with each partner leading with their own family. This gives you the actual words for the situation, a plan to align with your partner first, and a way to hold the line when the guilt-trips come.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The real-issue read** — what's actually bothering you (unannounced visits, unsolicited advice, overstepping on the kids, comments) and what you need to change
- **A warm-but-firm script** — the specific words: appreciation + the boundary + the ask, without apology or attack
- **A partner-alignment plan** — agreeing the boundary together first, and who delivers it (ideally each to their own family)
- **Holding the line** — responses to the pushback, guilt-trips, and "we've always done it this way"
- **De-escalation** — keeping it about the behavior and the future, so it protects the relationship rather than declaring war

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The issue** — the specific behavior and how often
- **Whose family** — your in-laws or your own (affects who should lead)
- **The dynamic** — generally warm, tense, or already conflict-ridden
- **Where you and your partner stand** — are you aligned, or is your partner conflict-avoidant
- **Your goal** — change the behavior while keeping the relationship, or a firmer line

## Framework: United, Warm, Firm, Steady

1. **Name what you actually need.** Get specific about the behavior and the change — vague frustration produces vague, ignorable boundaries.
2. **Align as a couple first.** Agree the boundary together before raising it; a divided couple invites triangulation. Ideally each partner leads with *their own* parents.
3. **Warm + firm, no apology.** Lead with genuine appreciation, state the boundary clearly, make a concrete ask — without over-apologizing (which invites negotiation) or attacking.
4. **Expect and hold through pushback.** Guilt, hurt, and "you're overreacting" are common; a calm broken-record restatement holds the line without a fight.
5. **Keep it about behavior and forward.** Focus on the specific behavior and what you'd like going forward, not a character indictment — this protects the long-term relationship.

## Output Format

### In-law boundary: issue [x] · whose family [y] · goal [z]

**What you need:** [the specific behavior change].
**Align first (with your partner):** [agree the boundary · who delivers it — ideally their own parent].

**Script**
> [Genuine appreciation] + [the boundary, clearly] + [the concrete ask]. — warm, firm, no over-apology.

**When they push back:** "[calm restatement]" — hold, don't debate.
**Keep it:** about the behavior and going forward, not their character.

## Quality Checks
- [ ] Pinpoints the specific behavior and the change wanted
- [ ] Includes aligning as a couple and who should deliver it
- [ ] Script is warm and firm, without over-apologizing or attacking
- [ ] Provides responses to hold the line against pushback
- [ ] Keeps focus on behavior/future, protecting the relationship
- [ ] Tailored to whose family and the existing dynamic

## Anti-Patterns
- **A divided couple** letting the family split them.
- **Vague hints** instead of a clear boundary.
- **Over-apologizing** so it reads as negotiable.
- **Character attacks** that torch the relationship.
- **Caving at the first guilt-trip** — no plan to hold the line.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "My mother-in-law drops by unannounced constantly — what do I say?"
- "Help me set a boundary with my in-laws about the kids."
- "My partner's family gives nonstop unsolicited advice. How do we handle it?"
- "How do my partner and I present a united front to their parents?"
- "What do I say when they guilt-trip me for setting a boundary?"
