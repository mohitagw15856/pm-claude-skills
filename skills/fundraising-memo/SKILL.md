# Fundraising Memo Skill

A deck is built to be presented; a memo is built to be read alone, forwarded, and argued about in a room the founder is not in. That makes it a harder and more honest document: there is no voice-over to cover a gap, and a claim without evidence stands out. Many investors now ask for one. This skill writes the memo from what the founder actually has. For slides, use `investor-pitch-deck`; for the questions and answers behind a round, use `fundraising-faq`; for updates to existing investors, use `investor-update`.

## What This Skill Produces

- **A memo of two to four pages** in connected prose, with a few tables where numbers belong
- **A one-paragraph version** for the email that carries it
- **An evidence list**: each claim in the memo and what supports it
- **The hard questions**: what a careful investor will ask, so the founder can prepare

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The company**: what it does, for whom, and the stage
- **The evidence**: revenue, usage, retention, pilots, waiting list, letters of intent, with dates
- **The team** and why they are suited to this problem
- **The round**: amount sought, instrument, what it pays for, and the milestone it reaches
- **The market**, with the sources for any size figure
- **What is not working yet.** A memo that hides it is weaker than one that names it

Use only the figures and facts supplied. Never invent a market size, a growth rate, a customer name or a comparable company's valuation. Where the founder gives a number without a source or a date, ask, or mark it as the founder's estimate.

## Framework: A Memo That Survives Being Forwarded

1. **Open with the whole case in one paragraph.** What the company does, the evidence it is working, and what is being raised. A reader who stops here should still be able to repeat it.
2. **State the problem through one customer.** A specific person, what they do today, and what it costs them. Then say how many such customers there are and how that count was reached.
3. **Explain why now.** What changed in technology, regulation, cost or behaviour that makes this possible or urgent this year.
4. **Describe what exists.** What has been built and shipped, in plain terms. Separate what works today from what is planned.
5. **Show the evidence in order of strength.** Money paid, then repeated use, then commitments, then interest. Give dates. Say what the numbers do not yet show.
6. **Lay out the business.** How money is made, what one customer is worth and costs to win, using supplied figures. If the unit economics are unproven, say what would prove them.
7. **Name the competition honestly**, including the option of doing nothing, and say what is hard to copy.
8. **Introduce the team** by what they have done that bears on this problem.
9. **Set out the plan.** The amount, how long it lasts, what it is spent on, and the milestone that makes the next round possible.
10. **Write the risks yourself.** The three or four that could sink the company and what is being done about each. Investors will list them anyway.
11. **State the ask** and the terms being proposed.

## Quality Checks

- [ ] The opening paragraph carries the whole case on its own
- [ ] Every figure has a date and a source, or is marked as an estimate
- [ ] What exists is separated from what is planned
- [ ] Risks are stated plainly, with a response to each
- [ ] The use of funds ties to a milestone
- [ ] No market size, comparable or customer was invented

## Anti-Patterns

- [ ] Do not paste the deck's bullets into paragraphs
- [ ] Do not quote a huge market number with no route from it to this company's customers
- [ ] Do not describe planned features in the present tense
- [ ] Do not claim there is no competition
- [ ] Do not leave the risks for the investor to find
