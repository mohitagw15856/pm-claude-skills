# Repair After a Fight

Every relationship has fights — what separates the strong ones isn't never fighting, it's *repairing* well afterward. Left unrepaired, the silence hardens and the resentment compounds. This helps you reconnect: figure out what actually needs mending, own your part specifically (not a defensive non-apology), acknowledge their hurt, and rebuild trust — because the repair is where the relationship is actually made or broken.

## What This Skill Produces

- **What needs repairing** — separating the surface incident from the deeper hurt underneath (often "you didn't have my back" matters more than the topic)
- **A genuine repair approach** — owning your specific part, acknowledging their feelings, and a real (not defensive or "sorry you feel") apology where warranted
- **The reopening words** — how to break the silence and start the repair without reigniting the fight
- **The rebuild** — moving from "okay we're past it" to actually restoring trust and understanding what happened
- **A both-sides note** — repair usually needs each person to own their part; how to do yours without demanding theirs

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The fight** — what it was about and what was said/done
- **Your part** — honestly, what you contributed (defensiveness here blocks repair)
- **The relationship** — partner, friend, family, and how it usually goes
- **Where it stands** — cold silence, a tense truce, or still raw
- **Your goal** — genuinely repair, or just end the standoff

## Framework: Reconnect, Own, Rebuild

1. **Find the real hurt.** The topic of the fight is often not the wound — underneath is usually feeling unheard, disrespected, or unsupported. Repair the real thing.
2. **Own your part specifically.** A genuine "I was wrong to X, and I can see it hurt you" repairs; a defensive "I'm sorry but you..." or "sorry you feel that way" makes it worse. Take your actual part.
3. **Acknowledge their hurt.** Show you understand how it landed for them, without rushing to defend or explain — feeling understood is most of repair.
4. **Reopen without reigniting.** Break the silence with warmth and ownership, not a re-litigation of who was right.
5. **Rebuild, don't just bury.** Move past "let's forget it" toward actually understanding what happened and how to handle it better — that's what restores trust.
6. **Do your part regardless.** You can own your side without them owning theirs; leading the repair often unlocks theirs, but don't make yours conditional.

## Output Format

### Repair with: [person] · the fight: [what happened] · now: [silence/tense/raw]

**What actually needs repairing:** [surface incident vs the deeper hurt].
**Own your part:** [the specific thing you did — genuinely, not defensively].
**Acknowledge their hurt:** "[show you get how it landed]".
**Reopen with:**
> [Warm, owning, not re-litigating — breaks the silence].
**Rebuild:** [understand what happened + how to handle it better] — not just "let's forget it".
**Your part, regardless:** do it without making it conditional on theirs.

## Quality Checks
- [ ] Distinguishes the surface incident from the deeper hurt
- [ ] Owns a specific part genuinely (no defensive non-apology)
- [ ] Acknowledges the other person's feelings
- [ ] Reopens warmly without re-litigating the fight
- [ ] Aims to rebuild trust, not just bury it
- [ ] Encourages doing one's own part unconditionally

## Anti-Patterns
- **A defensive "sorry but you..."** non-apology.
- **"Sorry you feel that way"** — the opposite of repair.
- **Rushing to move on** without understanding what happened.
- **Making your apology conditional** on theirs.
- **Re-litigating who was right** in the reopening.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "I had a bad fight with my partner — how do I make up?"
- "How do I repair things after an argument with my friend?"
- "We fought and now it's just cold silence. Help me reconnect."
- "I hurt someone in an argument and want to fix it."
- "What do I say to make up after a big blowup?"
