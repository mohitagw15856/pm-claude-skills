# Exam Study Plan

Most study plans fail two ways: they're a vague "revise everything" with no schedule, and they rely on re-reading and highlighting — which feel productive but barely work. This builds a backward-planned schedule from your exam date, focuses effort where it counts, and uses the methods that actually create retention (active recall, spaced repetition, practice testing) so you walk in prepared, not fried.

## What This Skill Produces

- **A backward-planned schedule** — week-by-week (and nearer the date, day-by-day) working back from the exam
- **Prioritized coverage** — weighted by what's most examined and where you're weakest, not equal time on everything
- **Effective methods** — sessions built on active recall, spaced repetition, and practice testing rather than passive re-reading
- **Practice integration** — past papers/mock exams worked in, especially close to the date, under realistic conditions
- **A sustainable pace** — study blocks, breaks, and buffer, so it's doable and doesn't collapse into a cram
- **A final-days plan** — light review and consolidation, not last-minute new material

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The exam** — subject, format (multiple choice, essay, problems), and date
- **Time available** — how many days/weeks and hours per day
- **The material** — topics/syllabus and their rough weighting
- **Your weak spots** — what you find hard or haven't covered
- **Your baseline** — how prepared you are now, and any practice resources (past papers)

## Framework: Plan Back, Study Actively, Test Often

1. **Work back from the date.** Lay out the weeks to the exam and allocate topics across them, with tighter scheduling near the end.
2. **Prioritize by weight and weakness.** Spend the most time on high-yield, heavily-examined topics and your weak areas — not evenly, not on what's already solid.
3. **Use methods that work.** Replace re-reading/highlighting with active recall (testing yourself), spaced repetition (revisiting over increasing intervals), and practice problems — these drive real retention.
4. **Practice under real conditions.** Integrate past papers/mocks, especially in the final stretch, timed and closed-book, to build exam readiness and surface gaps.
5. **Pace it sustainably.** Focused blocks with breaks, and a buffer for slippage — a plan you can keep beats an ideal one you abandon.
6. **Finish with consolidation.** The last days are for review and confidence, not cramming new material.

## Output Format

### Study plan: [exam/format] · date [x] · [hours/day available]

**Priorities:** [high-weight topics + your weak spots] get the most time.
**Schedule (backward)**
- Weeks out: [topic coverage with active recall].
- Mid: [continue + start practice].
- Final week: [past papers timed · consolidate weak spots].
- Last days: [light review, confidence, sleep — no new material].

**Methods each session:** active recall · spaced repetition · practice problems (not re-reading).
**Pace:** [blocks + breaks + buffer].

## Quality Checks
- [ ] Schedule is planned backward from the exam date
- [ ] Time is prioritized by topic weight and weak areas
- [ ] Sessions use active recall/spaced repetition/practice, not re-reading
- [ ] Past papers/mocks are integrated, timed, near the end
- [ ] Pace is sustainable with breaks and buffer
- [ ] Final days are consolidation, not new material

## Anti-Patterns
- **"Revise everything"** with no schedule or priorities.
- **Passive re-reading/highlighting** as the main method.
- **Equal time on all topics** regardless of weight/weakness.
- **No practice papers** until it's too late.
- **A cram plan** that burns out and forgets.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "I have a chemistry exam in 3 weeks — build me a study plan."
- "Help me revise for finals, I have five subjects."
- "Make a study schedule that actually helps me remember things."
- "How should I study for a multiple-choice licensing exam?"
- "I've got 10 days and a lot to cover — plan my revision."
