# Worked Example — Event Budget Plan

## The brief

A 300-person, one-day customer conference in Manchester, 14 October. Budget £95,000, described by the sponsor as "a target, but £100k is the hard ceiling". Venue booked (£18,000 hire, £12,000 minimum food-and-beverage spend, 22:00 curfew). Headcount confidence is moderate: 340 registered, and the previous event saw 82% attendance. Sponsorship of £15,000 is verbally agreed with one partner, contract unsigned. First time at this venue. Two suppliers are new.

**Minimum F&B risk:** at 240 attendees, catering spend is £10,080 against a £12,000 minimum — a **£1,920 shortfall payable regardless**. Included in the forecast below.

**Variance:** budget £95,000 · committed £57,600 · paid £13,700 · **forecast out-turn £91,816** (£78,196 + £11,700 contingency + £1,920 minimum-spend risk) · variance **−£3,184 against target**, £8,184 under the ceiling.

**Sponsorship of £15,000 is excluded** from this budget until the contract is signed. Treating verbal sponsorship as income is how events end up funding a shortfall from somewhere else.

## What this example demonstrates

- **Fixed and per-head are separated**, so the 240/300/340 sensitivity is arithmetic rather than a rebuild.
- **The attrition trap is priced.** The venue's £12,000 F&B minimum bites at low attendance — the exact scenario a headcount-optimistic budget misses.
- **Contingency is argued, not defaulted.** 15% is justified by three named uncertainties; the note that a repeat event would carry 8% shows the number is a judgement, not a habit.
- **Committed is tracked apart from paid.** £57,600 is already unrecoverable while only £13,700 has left the account.
- **Unsigned income stays out.** The single most common way an event budget lies to its owner.
