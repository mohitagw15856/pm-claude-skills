# Example: the-third-answer

## What this skill does

Pushes past the obvious answers to a question: names the textbook responses and sets them aside, then finds genuinely different angles by changing the frame, each judged honestly for whether it holds up.

## How to trigger it

### Example 1: a non-obvious growth idea

**You say:**
> "Don't give me the generic answer: how does a small B2B invoicing tool grow when we can't outspend anyone on ads?"

**Claude produces (excerpt):**

### Question: growth for a small invoicing tool with no ad budget

**The obvious answers (we're skipping these):** content/SEO, referral programme, freemium tier, partner with accountants.

**Past the obvious**
1. **The recipient angle.** Every invoice you send is seen by a non-customer: the client paying it. Make the receiving experience (one-click pay, clean record, download for their books) noticeably better than a PDF, with a quiet "sent with [product]" line. Your customers' invoices become the channel. Non-obvious because it treats the counterparty, not the user, as the audience. Holds up? Yes; the volume is already there and the cost is design, not spend.
2. **The off-season angle.** Invoicing pain peaks at tax deadlines; every competitor markets then too. Market hardest in the quiet months when a switch is actually feasible (nobody migrates tools in deadline week). Non-obvious because it breaks "market when demand peaks". Holds up? Partly; cheaper attention, but intent is lower, so it suits nurture not conversion.
3. **The firing angle.** Publish honest "you don't need us" guidance for sub-5-invoice freelancers, with a free template. Non-obvious because it sends people away. Holds up? Risky; works only if the segment genuinely churns unprofitably, which the numbers must confirm first.

**Worth pursuing:** the recipient angle first; it compounds with usage and nobody in the category does it well.

### Example 2: when the honest output is "the obvious answer is right"

**You say:**
> "Give me a non-obvious way to reduce our app's crash rate."

**Claude produces (excerpt):**

**The obvious answers (we're skipping these):** better crash reporting, fix the top crashers, add regression tests.

**Past the obvious**
1. **The deletion angle.** Rank features by crashes-per-use; the least-used, most-crashing feature may be worth removing, not fixing. Holds up? Yes, if usage data confirms.

**Honest verdict:** for crash rate specifically, the boring answer (triage the top 5 crashers; they are usually 80% of volume) beats every clever angle. The non-obvious ideas here are supplements. Surprising-and-useful loses to textbook-correct this time, and saying so is part of the job.

## Tips for best results

- State the constraint that makes the obvious answers unsatisfying; it steers where the new frames come from.
- Expect each idea to carry a holds-up verdict; ask for the reasoning when one says "risky".
- If the obvious answer survives, take it; the skill says so rather than forcing novelty.
