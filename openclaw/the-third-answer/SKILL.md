# The Third Answer

The first answer to any open question is the average of everything ever said about it — correct, safe, and forgettable. This skill deliberately names those obvious answers, sets them aside, and keeps going to the ideas most people never reach because they stopped at "good enough." The goal isn't cleverness for its own sake; it's the genuinely useful angle hiding past the generic ones.

## What This Skill Produces

- **The obvious answers, named** — the 2–4 things anyone (or any search) would say, listed so we explicitly *don't* settle for them
- **The non-obvious angles** — ideas found by continuing past the obvious, each from a genuinely different frame
- **Why each is non-obvious** — what assumption or default it breaks
- **A reality check** — which of the unconventional ideas actually hold up vs. which are just different for the sake of it

## Required Inputs

Ask for these if not provided:
- **The question or problem** — the open-ended thing you want a fresh take on
- **What you've already considered** — so we skip your obvious answers too
- **Constraints** — what's actually fixed vs. what everyone just assumes is fixed
- **The bar** — surprising-but-doable, or genuinely wild

## Framework: Name The Obvious, Then Keep Going

1. **List the obvious out loud.** Write the textbook answers explicitly — this is what stops us from unconsciously returning to them.
2. **Set them aside.** Treat every obvious answer as off-limits; the interesting territory starts where they end.
3. **Change the frame, not just the answer.** Re-ask the question from a different discipline, timescale, or actor — a new angle produces new answers, more thinking on the same angle doesn't.
4. **Chase the ones that feel slightly wrong.** The idea you almost dismissed is often where the value is; develop it instead of discarding it.
5. **Then judge honestly.** Separate the unconventional-and-useful from the unconventional-and-dumb — novelty without a reality check is noise.

## Output Format

### Question: [the problem]

**The obvious answers (we're skipping these):** [2–4 generic ones].

**Past the obvious**
1. **[Angle]** — [the idea]. Non-obvious because [assumption it breaks]. Holds up? [yes/partly/risky].
2. **[Angle]** — …

**Worth pursuing:** [the 1–2 that survive scrutiny].

## Quality Checks
- [ ] The obvious answers are explicitly named and set aside
- [ ] New ideas come from changed frames, not more of the same angle
- [ ] Each non-obvious idea says what default it breaks
- [ ] A genuine reality check separates useful from merely-different
- [ ] The output would make someone go "oh — I hadn't thought of that"

## Anti-Patterns
- **Dressing up the obvious answer** in fancier words and calling it novel.
- **Different for the sake of different** with no reality check.
- **Stopping at three ideas** when the good one was the fifth.
- **Ignoring real constraints** while breaking fake ones.

## Example Trigger Phrases
- "Give me a non-obvious angle on pricing my product."
- "Don't give me the generic answer — how should I actually grow this?"
- "What's an idea for this problem that nobody else would suggest?"
- "Think outside the box on how to spend my Saturday."
- "Skip the textbook answer — what's the interesting take?"
